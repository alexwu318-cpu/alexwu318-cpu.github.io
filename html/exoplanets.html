Worlds Beyond Our Solar System

Planets we've never seen, proven by the light they steal.

An exoplanet is any planet orbiting a star other than the Sun. We can't photograph most of them directly — instead, scientists catch the fingerprints they leave on starlight. Over 5,900 have been confirmed so far.

How We Find Them

Three ways to catch an invisible planet

Transit Method

Watching a star blink

A regular, repeating dip in a star's brightness means something is crossing in front of it. The size of the dip tells us the planet's size — this is how roughly 70% of known exoplanets were found.

Radial Velocity

Catching a star's wobble

A planet's gravity tugs its star into a tiny wobble. That wobble stretches and squeezes the star's light — a Doppler shift we can measure from Earth, even without seeing the planet at all.

Direct Imaging

Photographing a planet's own light

Rare and difficult: blocking out a star's glare to capture a planet's own faint glow directly. It works best for young, bright, widely-orbiting giants far from their star.

Featured Worlds

Two planets worth knowing

Kepler-186f

The first Earth-sized planet in a habitable zone

Fun FactAt high noon on Kepler-186f, its star would look only as bright as our Sun about an hour before sunset.
Kepler-186f was the first validated Earth-size planet found orbiting squarely inside its star's habitable zone — the range of distance where liquid water could pool on a surface. It orbits a small, cool red dwarf star about 500 light-years away in the constellation Cygnus, completing one orbit every 130 days. Its size is known, but its mass, composition, and atmosphere remain a mystery.
WASP-12b

A planet being slowly devoured

Fun FactWASP-12b orbits its star once every 26 hours and is losing mass to it every second.
WASP-12b is a scorching gas giant orbiting so close to its star that tidal forces have stretched it into an egg shape, and the star is slowly stripping away its atmosphere. It's also the first known carbon-rich planet — with more carbon than oxygen, some scientists think it could hide diamond deep in its interior.
