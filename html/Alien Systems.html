Beyond Single-Star Skies

Some skies have two sunsets — or three, or more.

Most stars in our galaxy aren't alone. Roughly half of all Sun-like stars belong to binary or multiple-star systems, orbiting a shared center of gravity. Any planets in these systems would see something no human ever has: more than one sun in the sky.

Kinds of Multi-Star Systems

Four ways a sky can hold more than one sun

Binary Systems

Two stars, one shared center of gravity

Two stars locked in mutual orbit around a shared center of mass — the most common type of multiple-star system in the galaxy.

Circumbinary Planets

Orbiting both stars at once

Planets that circle a binary pair from the outside, like real-world "Tatooine" worlds. Kepler-16b, confirmed in 2011, is the best-known example.

Circumstellar Planets

Orbiting just one star of the pair

Instead of circling both stars, some planets orbit only one, with the second star appearing as a bright, distant companion in the sky.

Trinary & Higher Systems

Three or more stars, kept stable by hierarchy

Systems like Alpha Centauri pair two stars closely while a third orbits much farther out — the arrangement that keeps such systems gravitationally stable.

Featured Systems

Two systems worth knowing

Alpha Centauri

Our closest neighboring system

Fun FactThis photo isn't an illustration — Cassini really captured both Alpha Centauri A and B glowing above Saturn's horizon in 2008.
Alpha Centauri is a triple system just 4.2 light-years away, made up of Alpha Centauri A and B — both Sun-like stars — and the faint red dwarf Proxima Centauri. From Saturn's orbit, this Cassini image shows how distinctly the two brighter stars still stand out, even though their light takes over four years to reach us.
Kepler-16b

The real "Tatooine"

Fun FactKepler-16b's two stars eclipse each other from our point of view, which is how scientists measured them so precisely.
Kepler-16b is a cold, gaseous planet orbiting a close binary pair — a K dwarf about 69% the Sun's mass, and a red dwarf about 20%. It orbits its stars once every 229 days, at a distance comparable to Venus's orbit around our Sun, but stays frigid since both parent stars are far dimmer than ours.
